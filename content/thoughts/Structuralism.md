Language existed before we learned to speak it. We can follow its rules without being able to explain them. Lévi-Strauss takes that possibility into anthropology: people may participate in a kinship system without knowing the rules an anthropologist uses to describe it.

The unit of explanation is a relation within a system. Calling someone an uncle tells us little about the obligations between that person and a nephew. We need the other relationships, the prescribed conduct, and the cases where conduct departs from the kinship vocabulary. In _Structural Anthropology_, chapter II, Lévi-Strauss distinguishes the terms for relatives from the attitudes and duties associated with them.[^structure]

## what a structural explanation has to explain

A structural model specifies how its elements depend on one another and how those relations change under a transformation. Lévi-Strauss distinguishes this model from the observed social relations used to construct it. Giving a custom a place in a diagram still leaves the explanation to be tested.[^structure]

His Oedipus example makes the method easier to inspect. He groups episodes by relations: Oedipus marrying his mother and Antigone burying her brother enter a group he reads as excessive attachment to blood relations; killing a father or brother enters the opposing group. This is part of his proposed analysis, with an explicitly provisional arrangement of the evidence.[^myth]

Lévi-Strauss treats [[thoughts/Freud|Freud]]'s reading of Oedipus as another variant of the myth. His comparison includes modern psychoanalytic retellings alongside the ancient versions.[^myth]

The difficult step is choosing the relations. A persuasive reading has to account for the variants it encounters and explain why its grouping is warranted. A claimed universal structure of thought needs evidence across cases. The existence of shared rules alone leaves that claim open.

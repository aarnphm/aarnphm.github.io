### Hegel's dialectics

Hegel follows a position until its own commitments force it to change. This movement is **immanent**: the difficulty arises from what the position claims, using standards it already accepts.

In [[library/The Phenomenology of Spirit]], the question concerns [[thoughts/Epistemology|knowledge]]. Consciousness distinguishes its knowledge from what it takes the object to be in itself. It then tests whether the two agree. When that test fails, its account of the object and its standard of knowing can change together ([Introduction, §§84–87](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hegel/works/ph/phintro.htm)).

Hegel calls the result **determinate negation** (§79). The failure has a specific content, so the next position retains something learned from the one it overturns. To explain a transition, we need to show what failed and how the successor follows from that failure.

Take his [sense-certainty example, §§95–97](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hegel/works/ph/phaa.htm). Sense-certainty claims to know a particular immediately. Write down "Now is night" and read it at noon. The particular night has passed, while "now" still applies. Hegel argues that the word expresses a universal capable of taking different contents. The attempt to state immediate knowledge of this particular has brought a universal into the account. This is one step in his argument; the passage depends on the claim that immediate certainty can express its truth.

In [[thoughts/logic|logic]], Hegel examines thought-determinations themselves. The opening treatment of [being, nothing, and becoming (§§86–88)](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hegel/works/sl/slbeing.htm) asks what content remains in pure being once every determination is excluded. Each proposed transition needs that examination of its terms.

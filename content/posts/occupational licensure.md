> [!question]-
>
> The Professional Engineers Act gives provincial regulators such as Professional Engineers Ontario (PEO) the responsibility to
> regulate the registration of professional engineers within their mandated jurisdictions. On May 15 2023, PEO implemented
> new changes in the process for one to become licensed as a Professional Engineer in Ontario. [^links]
>
> After your review of the Professional Engineers Act, and the licensing procedure, do you think this new procedure
> strengthens the engineering profession? Does it support what the Professional Engineers Act stands
> for? Is it equitable for engineers whose educational background is not considered 'traditional'?
> For example one who’s undergraduate degree is not in engineering but have a graduate degree in engineering,
> or are internationally educated engineers?

[^links]: See also [Professional Engineers Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90p28) and [PEO Procedure](https://www.peo.on.ca/apply/licensing-changes#:~:text=applicants%20%E2%80%93%20PEO%20has%20launched%20an,Limited%20Licence%20in%20the%20future)

According to [@friedman1962capitalism{chap. IX}], while justified as protecting consumers from fraudulent practitioners, occupational licensure “almost inevitably becomes a tool in the hands of a special producer group to obtain a monopoly position at the expense of the rest of the public.” The Act's stated object is the opposite: PEO regulates the practice of professional engineering “in order that the public interest may be served and protected” (s. 2(2)). Read against Friedman, the May 15 update mostly moves in the right direction. It removes the barrier that fell hardest on internationally educated engineers and judges experience by competency rather than by where it was gained. This essay argues that the changes strengthen the profession and serve the Act, but that they leave the academic gate untouched, and that gate is where engineers with non-traditional backgrounds are still treated inequitably.

### What changed

The May 15 changes removed the Canadian experience requirement, moved every applicant to a competency-based assessment of the 48 months of experience, closed the Engineering Intern program to new applicants, and required applicants to meet the academic and experience requirements before applying.[^peo] PEO also committed to a registration decision within six months for 90% of complete applications.

The first two changes are the ones that matter for equity. A rule that some of your experience must be Canadian is a rule about geography, not about competence, and it fell almost entirely on internationally educated engineers. A competency-based assessment asks what an applicant can do, which is the question a public-interest regulator should be asking. On these points the update is what Friedman's critique would recommend, and it strengthens the profession by tying the licence more closely to the skill it is supposed to certify.

The other two changes are smaller. Closing the EIT program removes a paid interim status rather than a requirement, since experience still counts toward the 48 months whether or not the applicant was registered as an intern. Requiring applicants to be fully qualified before applying lengthens the time before a new graduate has any standing with PEO, but it does not raise the bar they have to clear.

### Where it is still inequitable

The academic requirement did not change: a CEAB-accredited bachelor's degree, or a bachelor's degree on PEO's recognised programs list plus confirmatory exams. It may still exclude those who hold graduate degrees in engineering but do not have an undergraduate degree in the field, and internationally educated engineers whose programs are not on the list, who may have equivalent or even superior training. Someone with a physics bachelor's and an engineering master's has demonstrated more engineering education than the standard applicant, yet the requirement is written around the undergraduate degree. %% check: confirm how PEO currently treats a graduate engineering degree in the academic assessment (technical exams assigned case by case?). the essay should state the actual rule. %%

By defining "qualified" through one kind of degree, PEO risks excluding individuals who may bring valuable perspectives and skills to the profession. This is the part of the system that Friedman's argument still applies to. Requiring permission from a governing body to work in one's chosen occupation restricts [[thoughts/Camus#absurd freedom|individual freedom]], and when the requirement tests a credential rather than a competence, the restriction protects incumbents more than it protects the public [@friedman1962capitalism]. An academic gate that is stricter than the competence it stands for can create an artificial scarcity of engineers, raise the cost of engineering projects, and push capable people toward jurisdictions where they can more easily practise. %% check: the original claimed Canada has an engineering shortage and that engineers leave for the US because of licensing. both need a source (Engineers Canada labour-market reports would be the place to look), or they should stay as possibilities, as written here. %%

### What would serve the Act better

Maintaining high professional standards is essential, but licensing procedures should not exclude capable engineers. Friedman advocated certification systems that would allow consumers to identify qualified practitioners while not preventing others from practising. PEO has already taken the first step he would recommend for experience. The consistent next step is to apply the same principle to academics: assess what an applicant knows, through exams or a competency review open to any degree path, rather than which program granted the degree, and recognise different levels of expertise through tiered certification. That would keep the standard the Act asks for while opening the profession to the engineers the question asks about.

[^peo]: PEO, [Licensing changes](https://www.peo.on.ca/apply/licensing-changes), effective May 15, 2023. The Canadian experience change followed Ontario's _Working for Workers Act, 2021_, which bars Canadian experience requirements in regulated professions.

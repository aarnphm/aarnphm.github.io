> I, **Aaron Pham**, in the presence of these my betters and my equals in my Calling, bind myself upon my Honour and Cold Iron, that, to the best of my knowledge and power, I will not henceforward suffer or pass, or be privy to the passing of, Bad Workmanship or Faulty Material in aught that concerns my works before mankind as an Engineer, or in my dealings with my own Soul before my Maker. My time I will not refuse; my Thought I will not grudge; my Care I will not deny towards the honour, use, stability and perfection of any works to which I may be called to set my hand.

Kipling's archaisms are there to give this document a 17th-century weight which it does not actually have: he composed it in 1922, at H. E. T. Haultain's request, three years before the first ceremony. The ceremony remains semi-secret, open only to engineers and engineers-to-be. The communitas wrought by this in the room is real enough but can best be characterized as a feeling. Emotivism, then, lies close at hand: feelings are what emotivism is most interested in.

---

![[posts/images/quebec-bridge-collapse.webp|Remains after the southern span fell in 1907, killing 75.]]

The clause I keep returning to is _or be privy to the passing of_. In 1907 the chords of the Quebec Bridge were visibly bending, the consulting engineer in New York was told, and his telegram to stop loading the span was still being discussed when it fell.

And there's the phenomenology of a ring actually being worn. The thing itself is nothing to write home about: it is not a ring that makes a remarkable impression on the hand it adorns. The distinctive sound of its click onto my table begins to disappear. After about four months of wear, the iron ring is just another piece of jewelry in a ring box, and the half-amused tap I give it each morning when I put it on is just another doodle on a napkin.

The whole affair is meant to have a certain effect, namely, to nudge me into a gradually deepening self-consciousness about the ethical nature of things. [[thoughts/moral|Moral]] utterances are ultimately expressions of attitude; they cannot be reduced without loss to statements of fact (as Ayer and Stevenson long ago insisted). I wanted to conclude from this that the scheme fails, that no sound on a piece of jewelry is going to shift my moral sensibilities. The inference runs the other way. If moral language is attitude, then ritual and repetition are exactly the things that shift it, and Stevenson's whole account of ethical words is about their pull on other people's attitudes.[^stevenson]

What the ceremony really does is create a sudden warm sense of tribal belonging: murmured sentiments, punctilious recitations, the performance of communal grammatical forms. Particularly when the archaic words are followed by the Obligation's punchy line, it produces the happy feeling of belonging to something bigger than oneself. The ethical core of what's being achieved is in the shared feeling the recitations produce, the feeling of belonging to a community. In the language game of the engineering profession, the ethical sense relies on _blik_, [Hare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._M._Hare)'s name for that definite though unfounded sense of how things really are.

---

I am aware now, past the midpoint in the first post-graduate summer, that after five years the word student has begun to fall away from me as the default position from which to run meaning off my actions. It's one thing to drop the title 'student'; it's something else that the roles that go with it, the other people in similar sets of clothes, the tiny rituals and screams and class distinctions that make all the difference to how we live, should be quietly dissolved when the course actually ends. It's odd to miss Exam Week now, even in memory. It is odd, too, to recognize that one's convivial relationships with fellow students in university were primarily sustained by shared obligations, and that those obligations served with delicate economy as the fragile scaffold of a communal life.

But this feeling of oddness is nothing compared to that of my luggage on the GO train to Hamilton the morning of the ceremony itself, riding past the trees and fields and the glittering lakes and rivers. Convocation serves as a ready-made narrative symbolisation, a figuration of closure. It has no bearing on reality.

The ceremony attempts to generate meaning in the here-and-now practices of the institution through commitment to a rule-governed [[thoughts/forms of life|form of life]]. In Wittgenstein's words:

> If I have exhausted the justifications I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am inclined to say: 'This is simply what I do.' [@wittgenstein1953philosophical]

This seems a reasonable way to describe graduation. University education is nothing if not an immersion in many and varied language games, engineering for one thing, but also ethics, and aesthetics, and social rituals of all sorts, each of which has its particular rules governing how we speak and act among others. The point is that we learn to speak and act according to these rules, not that we ever succeed in justifying them.

I lost sight of the ring on my hand for a month. Much to my surprise, much to my own inexplicable relief, what endured was a quiet feeling that something is wrong about not seeing it: a connection not only with an obligation I must not forget but with a tribe and its practices, the form of life that made the notion of obligation intelligible to me in the first place.

Thus I hold my ring out in front of me. It is not quite clean, because I haven't had much time to clean it; and it is not quite what I thought I would be holding in my hand when I finally looked back at myself and my life to this point, but then again it is.

[^stevenson]: A. J. Ayer, _Language, Truth and Logic_ (1936), ch. 6; C. L. Stevenson, "The Emotive Meaning of Ethical Terms," _Mind_ 46 (1937), where ethical words have a "magnetism" that works on the hearer's attitudes.

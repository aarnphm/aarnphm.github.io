ad abundantiam

i gave claude the spec. twenty minutes later, the tests passed and the thing worked. i expected relief. instead i felt a blankness where relief normally goes. i had skipped the work between wanting the project and receiving it.

this is the part of abundance i did not expect. more things have become possible for me. fewer of them feel necessary.

## completion without work

i use _hyperabundance_ for the point where my options grow faster than i can compare or act on them. it is a personal condition, rather than a forecast about post-scarcity. i can start more projects, learn from more people, and reach more information than i could use in a lifetime.

cheap completion changes the relation between a wish and its result. the old project mattered partly because i had failed to finish it. it represented six months of distraction, embarrassment, and unfinished intention. claude removed the technical obstacle while leaving that history untouched. the result worked, yet it felt detached from the person who had begun it.

the missing part was participation. effort would have forced hundreds of small decisions. those decisions would have changed the project and trained my judgment. the completed artifact gave me the answer while removing most of the time in which the answer could have become mine.

this does not make friction good by itself. a broken tool, a long queue, and a bad API can waste a day without teaching me anything. the useful friction is the part that requires attention and leaves a trace in my taste. when machinery removes that part, i gain an output and lose a chance to become the person who could have made it.

## possibility and necessity

kierkegaard gives me a useful description of the feeling. in _The Sickness unto Death_, he describes the self as getting lost in possibility when nothing becomes actual.[^kierkegaard] possibility needs necessity, some fact that limits the field and gives the self a place to return to.

the claim becomes concrete at a desk. i open one project, notice that another looks easier, ask an agent to finish a third, and end the day with several results that never required my full attention. switching is cheap enough that no task gets to make a claim on me. my list grows. my ability to care stays finite.

every choice gives up alternatives for a while. that cost is part of what lets a choice shape a life. writing this note means leaving another file unopened. learning one system means accepting ignorance about ten others. staying with a person means letting some imagined futures remain imaginary.

abundance cannot choose among those futures for me. better search and faster execution only expose more of them. the decision still has to come from a commitment that survives the next attractive option.

## chosen constraint

![[posts/images/the-embrace-egon-schiele.webp|The Embrace, Egon Schiele (1917)]]

the two bodies in schiele's _the embrace_ press into each other, arms folded around backs and shoulders. i read the pose as dependence. the painting was made in 1917. edith schiele died from influenza in october 1918 while pregnant, and egon died from the same disease three days later.[^schiele]

that history proves nothing about the painting. it changes how i see the embrace. each person can be lost because each person remains outside the other. love receives its weight from that exposure and from the choices made in response to it.

the same answer applies to work. when the world stops imposing a narrow path, i have to choose one and remain there long enough for it to alter me. a person, a craft, a place, or a problem becomes necessary through repeated attention. other options remain possible. the commitment matters because i renew it while knowing that.

[^schiele]: The Leopold Museum dates Edith Schiele's death to October 28, 1918, and Egon Schiele's death to October 31. See [“Changing Times. Egon Schiele's Last Years”](https://www.leopoldmuseum.org/en/press/news/1403/Changing-Times-Egon-Schieles-Last-Years).

[^kierkegaard]: Søren Kierkegaard, _The Sickness unto Death_, translated by Howard V. Hong and Edna H. Hong, Princeton University Press.
